A study through the book of Nehemiah

Arise and Build

by Mark Emery
Installment Two · Nehemiah 2:11–3:32

Before the First Stone Is Laid

Nehemiah has arrived. After months of prayer and fasting in Susa, after a difficult audience with Artaxerxes, after a journey of perhaps a thousand miles through the Persian Empire — he is finally in Jerusalem. The broken walls he has been praying about for months are now right in front of him.

And he waits three days before doing anything about them.

This is the first lesson the opening verses of this passage teach: the man most consumed with a God-given vision is often the man most willing to be patient with its execution. Nehemiah rests. He settles. He takes in his surroundings. There is wisdom in this that runs against our instincts. We tend to assume that urgency equals speed, that caring deeply means acting immediately. But Nehemiah seems to understand something the book of Proverbs puts plainly: "Prepare your work outside and make it ready for yourself in the field; afterwards, then, build your house" (Prov 24:27).

The preparation matters as much as the work itself.

The wisdom of looking before speaking

When Nehemiah finally does act, he does so in secret. Taking only a few men and a single mount, he goes out at night through the Valley Gate and makes a full circuit of Jerusalem's broken walls. He says nothing to the officials, the priests, the nobles — no one knows where he has gone or what he has done.

Why the secrecy? The enemies of the returning exiles had been effective before in shutting down rebuilding efforts with accusations of rebellion. Nehemiah cannot afford to have his plans leaked before they are solid. But there is something else going on as well. He needs to know the full truth of the situation before he can speak to it honestly. He will not call the people to work before he himself has seen, with his own eyes, exactly what the work requires.

There is a time for silent, prayerful contemplation, and a time to speak. Nehemiah is able to discern between the two.

The text notes quietly that the rubble in the Kidron Valley was so severe his mount could not pass — the scale of the destruction had forced the walls of the city to be rebuilt on the crest of the hill rather than on their original slope. He has looked at the ruins honestly, all of them, and in the dark. This is the pattern of a careful leader: survey before you speak, assess before you address, see clearly before you say anything.

The speech that names and calls

Only then does he speak. And what he says to the people of Jerusalem is striking in its simplicity: "You see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lies waste, and its gates are burned with fire." He does not soften it. He does not reframe it. He names it for exactly what it is.

This is more important than it might seem. The people of Jerusalem have been living among these ruins for years. Broken walls had become simply the way things were. When something broken becomes familiar enough, it stops registering as broken — we learn to step over the rubble on the way to other things. Nehemiah's role in this moment is to make the familiar visible again, to say out loud what everyone could see but no one was saying: this is not acceptable. This is not what we are meant to be.

The first step toward rebuilding anything is refusing to pretend it isn't broken.

But he does not stop at naming the problem. He immediately connects it to something larger: the reproach on the people is a reproach on God himself. When Israel was called to be a kingdom of priests and a light to the nations, the condition of Jerusalem was not merely a civic embarrassment — it was a theological statement about the God who had made his name dwell there. The shame of the city and the shame of the God of the city were bound together.

And so Nehemiah calls them to act — not "I will build" but "Come and let us build." He takes the burden personally even as he distributes the labor broadly. Then he gives them grounds for confidence: the hand of his God had been favorable to him. He offers not theory but testimony. The response is immediate: "Let us rise up and build." And they put their hands to the work.

The opposition that always comes

It arrives almost immediately. Sanballat, Tobiah, and a new voice — Geshem the Arab — mock them and accuse them of rebellion against the king. The tactic is familiar: ridicule the work, impugn the motives, invoke the threat of consequences. Virtually encircled now by hostile neighbors to the north, east, and south, the people of Judah face what one commentator calls "the war of nerves."

Nehemiah's answer is spare and confident: "The God of heaven himself will prosper us; therefore we his servants will arise and build." He appeals not to the king's letters — though he has them — but to a higher authority. Then he draws a clear line: "You have no heritage or right or memorial in Jerusalem." This is not your work. You have no claim here.

There is what one writer rightly calls a "holy stubbornness" in Nehemiah's response. He is not rattled. He is not defensive. He simply states what is true and returns to the task. The people of God, when they know whose they are and what they have been called to do, need not be shaken by voices that only know how to mock and accuse.

Shoulder to shoulder — and one quiet note

Chapter three is sometimes passed over as a list of names and assignments. That would be a loss. It is one of the most humanly compelling chapters in all of Nehemiah. Person after person, family after family, guild after guild — goldsmiths and perfumers, priests and Levites, district leaders and ordinary residents — each one taking their assigned section of the wall and getting to work.

Notice what the text repeats like a refrain: "next to him," "next to them," "after him." The rebuilding of the wall is a shoulder-to-shoulder enterprise. No one builds their section alone, and no section stands disconnected from the one beside it. The wall is only as strong as the sum of its parts, and every part depends on someone showing up to build it. Notably, several builders are described as building "in front of his own house" — they are not only investing in the community, they are literally protecting their own homes while protecting everyone else's.

The wall that gets built is built by people who show up. The record of who built it — and who didn't — is written down.

And there is that one quiet note, slipped into verse 5 without elaboration: the Tekoites worked — but "their nobles did not put their shoulders to the work of their Lord." Not the work of the Persian king. The work of their Lord. The text does not condemn at length. It simply records it. In a chapter full of names honored for their faithful labor, a handful of names are conspicuously absent from the wall. The noting of it, amid so much affirmation of those who did build, is enough.

What this means for us

Nehemiah's approach to the work before him models something every congregation needs: honest assessment before confident action. He looks at what is broken before he says a word about building. He surveys before he speaks. He gives people the truth before he gives them a task.

The call he extends — "Come and let us build" — is a call to shared ownership. The walls belong to everyone. The reproach belongs to everyone. And so does the work of rebuilding. Chapter three shows us what it looks like when a community answers that call: hundreds of individuals, side by side, each carrying their section, together making something that none of them could have made alone.

We are not the first generation of God's people to look at something broken and have to decide what to do about it. The question Nehemiah puts to Jerusalem is the same one put to every community of faith in every generation: now that you see it, what will you do?

Questions for reflection

  1. Is there something in my walk with God that has become so familiar I've stopped seeing it clearly?
  2. What does Nehemiah's pattern of private assessment before public speech say to me about how I tend to approach difficult situations?
  3. What more can I do to "build the section in front of my own house" — to take ownership of my community's health and witness?
  4. Where in my life am I putting my shoulder to the work of the Lord — not when it's convenient, but consistently?
